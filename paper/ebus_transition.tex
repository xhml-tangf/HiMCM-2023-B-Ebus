\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Electrifying a Metro Bus Fleet Without Reducing Service}
\end{center}

\textbf{Question and baseline.} We study conversion of TransLink's fixed-route bus service to fully electric operation by 2033, with every 2023 departure, peak passenger capacity and accessible service preserved. Existing trolleybuses already count as electric. The comparator is ordinary renewal of the same service, not continued use of worn-out buses.

\textbf{Model.} A multi-year fleet replacement model links purchases and retirements to exact trip and charging feasibility. Each candidate must satisfy vehicle-class compatibility, departure times, combined passenger capacity, winter battery reserve, deadhead travel, spare buses and depot electrical limits. Gross discounted cost is kept separate from agency payment after 0\%, 25\% or 50\% grants. Environmental scoring uses matched life-cycle greenhouse-gas boundaries. Only feasible candidates belong on the cost--emissions Pareto frontier.

\textbf{Observed result.} Official route data contain 191 routes and 5,466,190.5 hours, covering 98.035\% of independently reported 2023 fixed-route bus hours. Actual service totals are 106,868,324 km and 5,715,791,401 capacity km after SeaBus is excluded. December reporting identifies 280 trolley/BEB buses combined; exact vehicle ages, blocks and site power remain unresolved. City-wide 2033 feasibility and Pareto scores remain \emph{unverified}.\cite{budget2025,noticeDec}

\textbf{Critical technology and planning finding.} A historical TransLink battery-bus scenario uses 0.02 L/km of fuel for cabin heat and fails the problem's strict fully electric definition. At 68,000 km/year, that is 1,360 L of heater fuel. Later named budgets also plan 203 CNG/diesel replacement vehicles across distinct cohorts; if delivered, those vehicles require accelerated withdrawal for a strict 2033 endpoint.\cite{fleet2020,budget2025,budget2026}

\textbf{Solved planning result.} London's registration cohorts yield a numerical ten-year benchmark: retaining the original available fleet by body type, a 17-year chassis-life scenario needs annual renewal capacity of at least 776 original vehicle positions; a 14-year scenario needs 977. Both integer-program optima equal an independently derived deadline bound. Route and charging checks determine additional vehicles. Separately, 243 charging LP cases show how grid capacity changes tariff choice: a conditional 100-distance-equivalent case needs at least 4.140 MW for an eight-hour window; its overnight electricity bill is 32.96\% below general service, before capital.

\textbf{Recommendation.} Treat 2033 as an accelerated target to test against TransLink's published 2040 goal. Obtain the missing block and depot data, retain trolley service, convert proven standard-bus blocks first, and require all-electric winter trials before committing double-deck and shuttle blocks.\cite{fleet2020,playbook2024}

\textbf{Keywords:} electric bus; fleet replacement; service preservation; charging; life-cycle cost.

\section{Problem definition and study boundary}
The competition asks for environmental and financial consequences, a ten-year transition in three metropolitan areas, and a decision-maker letter.\cite{problem} We take 31 December 2023 as Vancouver's decision baseline and plan purchases and construction in 2024--2033. Documents published in 2024--2026 inform later sensitivity checks, but their realized purchases are not backdated into 2023 decisions. Metro Vancouver is the primary case; its census metropolitan area had 2,642,825 residents in 2021, exceeding the problem's 500,000-person threshold.\cite{vancouvercensus} Chicago and London test transferability using their separately dated 2023 fleet snapshots; London's March audit is not treated as December stock.

The problem's definition requires propulsion \emph{and accessories} to use a zero-emission electric source. Thus a battery bus with a diesel auxiliary heater is not fully electric in this study. TransLink's historical depot-BEB cost table assumes precisely such a heater at 0.02 L/km; its 1.4 kWh/km electricity figure cannot be carried over unaltered to an all-electric winter bus. Renewable diesel, renewable natural gas, hybrid and hydrogen fuel-cell buses may reduce emissions, but do not satisfy this strict endpoint. Already electric trolleybuses do, provided their operations remain feasible. TransLink itself has a 2040 zero-emission target, making the requested 2033 date an accelerated counterfactual.\cite{fleet2020,playbook2024}

The decision unit is year $y$, bus class $k$, depot $d$, trip $i$, vehicle $v$ and charger interval $t$. Classes include standard, articulated, highway coach, double-deck, community shuttle and trolley vehicles. We preserve 2023 fixed-route coverage and do not offset electrification cost by reducing service.

\section{Fleet and service model}
\subsection{Decision variables and normal-renewal baseline}
Let $x_{ykd}$ be electric buses purchased; $r_{ykda}$ conventional buses retired from age cohort $a$; $h_{ydq}$ charger positions of power type $q$ commissioned; and $z_{iv}$ equal one when bus $v$ performs baseline trip $i$. Let $c_{vt}$ denote charge energy and $e_{vt}$ battery energy. For the normal-renewal case, age-expired conventional buses are replaced with conventional equivalents while the \emph{same trip and passenger-capacity demand} is served. An electric candidate is evaluated only against this feasible comparator. Historical purchase expenditure of existing buses is sunk; early retirement enters through foregone residual service or sale value.

For each class and depot, stock obeys
\begin{equation}
 N^{p}_{y,k,d}=N^{p}_{y-1,k,d}+x^{p}_{y,k,d}-r^{p}_{y,k,d},\qquad p\in\{\mathrm{electric,conventional,trolley}\}.
\end{equation}
No diesel, hybrid, CNG or hydrogen propulsion or non-electric accessory may operate on any fixed-route trip in 2033. Retirement and purchase are not constrained one-for-one: needed vehicle counts emerge from schedules and spare ratios. A short-range vehicle may force additional buses, chargers or drivers.

\subsection{Hard service and technical constraints}
Every baseline trip is retained at its original route, direction and departure time. Multiple smaller buses may serve one departure when necessary, and each assigned class must be compatible with the route:
\begin{align}
 \sum_{v\in V_i}z_{iv}&\geq 1 &&(i\in I),\\
 \sum_{v\in V_i}Q_vz_{iv}&\geq Q_i^0 &&(i\in I),\\
 \sum_{i\in I_{r,s,t}}\sum_{v\in V_i}Q_vz_{iv}
   &\geq Q^{0}_{r,s,t}. &&
\end{align}
The final expression denotes capacity available at the original route--direction peak; the trip-level inequality protects each departure. A smaller bus can replace a larger one only if an additional vehicle on that departure restores the capacity and both vehicles pass scheduling, energy and site checks. Accessible trips require accessible vehicles; depot/class spare stocks are protected. If demand growth is modeled, it is applied equally to baseline and electric cases.

As a separate \emph{calibration check}, the assigned annual output should reproduce 5,575,772 fixed-route bus service hours, 106,868,324 service km and 5,715,791,401 capacity km for the 2023 reference service within documented differences in measurement definitions. The capacity-km subtotal is especially useful for detecting systematic undersupply across small-bus substitutions, but it cannot substitute for the departure and peak constraints above.\cite{budget2025}

\begin{samepage}
Each vehicle's assigned trips and charging intervals cannot overlap. Location-to-location deadhead times must fit between events, including departure from and return to its depot. Driving to or from service also removes a vehicle from the available depot spare count. For battery vehicles, energy follows
\begin{align}
 e_{v,t+1}
 &=e_{vt}+\eta_q c_{vt}
   -\epsilon_{vk}^{\rm traction,winter}(\ell_{it}+\ell^{\rm deadhead}_{vt})
   -E^{\rm accessories}_{vt},\\
 \underline{s}_vB_v&\le e_{vt}\le \bar{s}_vB_v.
\end{align}
\end{samepage}
Here $E^{\rm accessories}_{vt}$ includes all-electric cabin heating and other onboard equipment during trips, deadhead, charging and active layovers; the traction coefficient excludes that energy to prevent double counting. An unmeasured winter accessory load makes an electric schedule \texttt{UNVERIFIED}, not zero-energy. Battery capacity and passenger capacity are linked when battery weight reduces available seats or payload. Charger occupancy, power and opening year impose
\begin{equation}
 \sum_v u_{vdt}\le H_{dt},\quad \sum_v P_{vdt}\le P^{\rm grid}_{dt},\quad
 H_{dt}=0\ \mathrm{until\ commissioned}.
\end{equation}
Conventional baseline vehicles instead require a fuel budget, with jointly calibrated distance and elapsed-duty rates $a_v,b_v$ in a consistent fuel unit (L or kg):
\begin{equation}
 F_{v,t+1}=F_{vt}-a_v\ell_{vt}-b_v\Delta t,\qquad
 F_v^{\rm reserve}\le F_{vt}\le F_v^{\rm tank}.
\end{equation}
The implemented check assumes no refuelling within a duty; the time rate includes stationary consumption and must not duplicate the distance rate. Missing fuel data leave the normal-renewal comparator unverified. Such vehicles may serve intermediate years but cannot satisfy the strict 2033 endpoint.

Trolleybuses use a separate wired-network check. Each trip, deadhead and powered layover needs a certified connected path and an operating feeder. Concurrent demand must obey $\sum_{v\in V_{ft}}P_v\le P_f^{\rm firm}$, where firm power is available after other loads and reserve. The code conservatively reserves each vehicle's measured all-in peak draw on every feeder traversed for the entire moving segment; stationary accessories use their separate demand. A failure of this sufficient power certificate calls for finer feeder timing before declaring physical infeasibility. Missing path or feeder records leave existing trolley operation unverified, without assigning these vehicles fictitious batteries or buying their capacity again.

Depot bus parking capacity, charger footprint, charging efficiency, construction lead time and route-specific winter testing must also pass. A charger site may be the home depot or a route terminal, provided the location and travel-time checks pass. The accompanying validator checks the supplied trip, energy and site records, returning \texttt{UNVERIFIED} for missing essential inputs; it cannot certify a city-wide solution from time-bin averages.

\subsection{Optimization order}
For each scenario, first solve or test service feasibility. Second find the minimum gross-cost normal-renewal baseline. Third enumerate feasible electric strategies: depot charging, terminal fast charging, battery sizing, depot sequencing and additional buses. Fourth solve an $\epsilon$-constraint family $\min C$ subject to $G\le\epsilon$ to reveal the Pareto frontier. A displayed ``balanced'' option would be chosen only after that frontier and stakeholder weights are available. We do not present a fictional optimum from incomplete inputs. This joint approach follows the scheduling/fleet-size logic of Rogge et al., the multi-year replacement logic of Pelletier et al., and the multi-depot timing logic of Gkiotsalitis et al.\cite{rogge,pelletier,gkiotsalitis}

\section{Costs, funding and environmental accounting}
Let $K_y$ include bus and battery procurement (initial battery included in a quoted vehicle price), chargers, depot civil work, grid connection and distribution upgrades, construction/design, training and any extra buses. Let $O_y$ include electricity, fuel, maintenance, replacement batteries, drivers, demand charges and regular charging-site upkeep. Let $S_y$ be realized salvage and $R_{2033}$ the remaining value of vehicles and durable infrastructure at the horizon. With a stated real discount rate $r$ and consistent price year,
\begin{align}
 C^{\rm gross}
 &=\sum_{y=2024}^{2033}\frac{K_y+O_y-S_y}{(1+r)^{y-2023}}
 -\frac{R_{2033}}{(1+r)^{10}},\\
 \Delta C&=C^{\rm gross}_{\rm electric}-C^{\rm gross}_{\rm renewal}.
\end{align}
For grant rate $f\in\{0,0.25,0.50\}$ and eligible expenditures $E_y$, $C^{\rm agency}=C^{\rm gross}-\sum_y fE_y/(1+r)^{y-2023}$. A grant changes who pays, not gross buses, copper, electricity or labour. Local employment and procurement impacts require an additional regional economic model and are reported separately to avoid double counting.\cite{usdot}

Define a life-cycle greenhouse-gas inventory over equal service:
\begin{align}
 G={}&G_{\rm vehicle+battery}+G_{\rm infrastructure}\\
 &+\sum_y\left(G_{\rm fuel,y}+G_{\rm electricity,y}
 +G_{\rm maintenance,y}\right)-G_{\rm end\ of\ life}.
\end{align}
Electricity uses an explicitly selected grid-intensity scenario. British Columbia's 2023 integrated-grid factor is 0.0113 kg CO$_2$e/kWh; it is an annual average and should be varied against marginal-generation possibilities.\cite{bcgrid} On-route NO$_x$, particles, electricity use and battery-production impacts appear in separate columns rather than being concealed by a single carbon score. If compatible manufacturing/infrastructure factors are absent, the \emph{life-cycle} score is unavailable even when operating emissions can be estimated.

The requested scores are
\begin{equation}
 S_C=100\frac{C_{\rm renewal}-C_{\rm electric}}{C_{\rm renewal}},\qquad
 S_G=100\frac{G_{\rm renewal}-G_{\rm electric}}{G_{\rm renewal}}.
\end{equation}
Negative values remain negative. The economic score is based on \emph{gross} cost, with subsidized agency cost shown beside it; otherwise the 50\% grant would artificially change the societal merit ranking.

\section{Observed and illustrative results}
The route table covers 98.035\% of independently reported actual fixed-route bus hours, and the source supplies actual annual bus service and capacity km. This passes stronger scale checks but not departure, block, winter energy or concurrent-charging checks, which require a missing vehicle-duty manifest. The 2023 full-city feasibility state is \texttt{UNVERIFIED}; no verified full-city score, purchasing schedule or Pareto set is issued.

For a transparent \emph{historical parameter demonstration}, the 2020 plan lists a 2019-CAD 40-foot diesel purchase of CAD 630,000, a depot BEB purchase of CAD 1,110,000, a 50-kW depot charger position of CAD 298,000, a 0.60 L/km diesel rate, a 1.4 kWh/km BEB electricity rate, 0.02 L/km of BEB heater fuel and 68,000 km per bus annually.\cite{fleet2020} One-to-one vehicle purchase premium is CAD 480,000; 68,000 km implies 40,800 L for diesel, versus 95,200 kWh \emph{plus 1,360 L heater fuel} for the historical BEB. At BC's 2023 \emph{average} factor, its electricity alone corresponds to about 1.076 t CO$_2$e/year. The BEB's heater makes it ineligible for the 2033 endpoint, and replacing that heater with electric heat changes winter energy demand. The CAD 298,000 charger quotation already includes design, permitting, project management, financing and a construction contingency; those items must not be charged again. This is neither a 2023 bid nor a complete cost/emissions comparison. TransLink's historical planning replacement ratio of 1.15 for depot charging shows why one-to-one replacement is unsafe.\cite{fleet2020}

For a separate \emph{electric-only 40-foot reference case}, the same plan models in-route charging at CAD 4.86/km, versus CAD 4.62/km for diesel, 4.77/km for hybrid and 4.15/km for CNG, all in 2019-CAD lifetime cost. The three alternative economic scores are $-5.19\%$, $-1.89\%$ and $-17.11\%$ respectively. The December 2020 update explicitly uses hybrid replacements as its conventional base case for retiring diesel/hybrid buses and CNG replacements for retiring CNG buses; the diesel comparison is a sensitivity reference. None is a fleet score: normal-renewal vehicle type, route assignment and replacement timing determine the actual denominator. Its in-route table specifies 1.6 kWh/km with no auxiliary fuel; using 0.60 L/km for diesel, the federal heavy-duty diesel combustion factor of 2.630 kg CO$_2$e/L and BC's 2023 average grid factor of 0.0113 kg CO$_2$e/kWh gives an \emph{operational-only} diesel-to-BEB GHG score of $98.85\%$ at identical distance. At 68,000 km/year, that is 106.07 t CO$_2$e/year of modeled operating savings per equivalent bus. Charging-site availability and actual 2023 blocks remain unverified; vehicle/battery/infrastructure manufacturing and fuel supply are excluded, so the requested full life-cycle city-wide environment score remains unavailable.\cite{fleet2020,fleetupdate2020,ghgmodule,bcgrid}

The 2024 ZEFTP offers a separate retrospective check. Its operating-and-maintenance costs are 3.30, 1.97, 2.77 and 2.28 CAD/vehicle-km for diesel, hybrid, CNG and BEB, respectively. On that \emph{O\&M-only} boundary, BEB scores are $+30.91\%$, $-15.74\%$ and $+17.69\%$ against the three alternatives; vehicle, battery and charger capital are absent. Its electric-heater BEB is reported at 19 g CO$_2$e/km, versus 1580 for fossil diesel, 1420 for fossil hybrid, 1350 for fossil CNG and 250 for renewable CNG. Fuel mix therefore changes the operational environmental comparator. These 2024 values are neither 2023-known procurement inputs nor full life-cycle 2024--2033 totals; they cannot be added to the older 2019-CAD costs.\cite{playbook2024}

An independent 2024 ICCT e-bus life-cycle method supplies transferable \emph{sensitivity factors}: 6.6 kg CO$_2$e per kg of vehicle glider/powertrain, 58 kg CO$_2$e per kWh of LFP battery, and 52.4/67.5 g CO$_2$e/km for diesel/BEB maintenance. Its 12--15 m example uses a 335-kWh battery and glider masses from Latin American buses, so these are not observed TransLink specifications. Its own system boundary excludes charging infrastructure and bus recycling. We retain separate terms for these missing components and do not substitute this partial method for a completed Vancouver life-cycle inventory.\cite{icct2024}

Facility timing is also consequential. A December 2020 program listed 677 planned BEB purchases and CAD 246.4 million of depot/charging investments, but subsequent 2024 review targeted Port Coquitlam for over 100 BEBs in 2027 and Marpole for roughly 340 in 2028. It estimated six to eight years to deliver charging at existing transit centres and recommended commissioning six to twelve months before bus delivery. These are planning and later-review quantities, not 2023 installed charger counts. The 2024 screening capital envelope includes service expansion and has a stated $-50\%$ to $+100\%$ accuracy range, so assigning it wholesale to our fixed-2023-service scenario would misstate incremental cost.\cite{fleetupdate2020,playbook2024}

The 2020 update provides a useful \emph{published-program arithmetic check}. Its Table 1 awards 449 depot-charged standard, 136 in-route-charged standard and 92 depot-charged articulated BEBs during 2020--2029, totaling 677 \emph{planned awards}, not deliveries. The reported incremental vehicle-purchase premium is CAD 236.9 million nominal; Table 3 adds CAD 246.4 million of depot/charging investment; and Table 5 projects CAD 96.3 million of cumulative operating savings through 2030 relative to its hybrid-renewal baseline. The resulting undiscounted component sum is CAD $236.9+246.4-96.3=387.0$ million. Annual operating cells rounded to CAD 0.1 million sum to CAD 96.1 million in savings, differing by CAD 0.2 million from the printed total; we use the printed total and retain the discrepancy in the audit. The 15 awards in 2020 were funded in an earlier program. The update anticipated electric purchases for retiring diesel/hybrid buses, yet the later 2025 plan lists 84 CNG buses for a 2024 hybrid retirement cohort; this is direct evidence that the older purchase pathway was revised. These source-period amounts cannot be rebased into 2024--2033 cost, grant or GHG scores: facility spending dates, later procurement revisions, 2023 fleet stock and all-electric winter duty feasibility are absent.\cite{fleetupdate2020,budget2025}

The historical depot analysis supplies a direct \emph{winter feasibility stress test}. With 660-kWh articulated batteries and fuel heaters, it projected depot-dependent replacement ratios of 1.13--1.55 and warned that electric-only heat could require roughly 10\% more buses. Applied mechanically to the later capital project's 39 retiring articulated diesels, $\lceil39(1.10)r_d\rceil$ gives 49--67 BEBs across hypothetical depot allocations, compared with its 45 planned depot-charged BEBs. The 4--22 difference is a \emph{historical-technology sensitivity}, not a measured shortage: the 39 actual depot assignments, 2026 energy specifications and block schedules are unknown. It identifies a concrete winter trial and procurement question before certifying the strict 2033 endpoint.\cite{fleet2020,budget2025}

\subsection{Charging tariffs and the value of firm power}
The original 2023 budget assumes diesel at CAD 1.66/L; this is a planning price, not an actual invoice. Its 32.17-million-L operational volume includes a broader system boundary and is not treated as fixed-route bus consumption.\cite{budget2023} BC Hydro's April-2023 tariff supplies energy, monthly demand and daily basic charges. RS1640 bills peak demand only during 06:00--22:00; RS1650's zero demand fee ends in March 2026 under that historical schedule. We therefore distinguish billed peak from physical grid power and do not extrapolate the temporary concession through 2033.\cite{bctariff2023}

For daily grid energy $E$, available charging hours $T$, site limit $P$, and rate-specific billed hours $T_j$, solve
\begin{equation}
 \min M_j:\quad \sum_{t\in T}p_t\Delta t=E,\quad
 0\le p_t\le P,\quad p_t\le M_j\ (t\in T_j).
\end{equation}
Power is zero outside $T$. If $h_0$ available hours are outside the billing window and $h_1>0$ are inside, the sharp lower bound is $M_j\ge\max\{0,(E-h_0P)/h_1\}$. Physical feasibility also requires $E\le(h_0+h_1)P$; a wholly nonbilled feasible window gives $M_j=0$. The executed CPU LP matches this independent bound in every feasible case. A second stage minimizes physical peak at the optimal billed peak. Monthly bills include energy, basic and demand charges, the prior-winter eleven-period minimum-bill floor, and the 1\% rider refund; taxes and capital are excluded.

We normalize to 100 \emph{distance-equivalent units}, each at the historical 68,000 km/year reference. These are not an observed fleet or a one-for-one replacement count. Historical onboard energy of 1.37--2.0 kWh/km, assumed charging efficiencies of 85/90/95\%, three common return windows and 2.5/3.5/5-MW site limits give 243 scenarios. Annual energy is spread evenly across 365 days; this relaxation does not certify winter energy, battery capacity, charger-port occupancy or actual bus availability. A meter-measured intensity would not be divided by efficiency again.\cite{fleet2020}

\subsection{A solved age-cohort planning benchmark}
For London, an original \emph{fleet slot} denotes one available vehicle position of the same body type. Renewing a slot requires suitable electric capacity; the operational model may require additional vehicles. Let $n_c$ be the observed size and $b_c$ the registration year of cohort $c$, and choose a maximum chassis-life scenario $L$. These caps are assumptions for sensitivity, not asserted TfL rules. A non-electric cohort must renew by $d_c=\min(2033,\max(2024,b_c+L))$. An existing electric cohort enters the renewal model only when $b_c+L\le2033$; the 2013 Routemaster retains its chassis vintage despite its 2022 electric retrofit.\cite{tflaudit}

Let integer $a_{cy}$ allocate original slots to renewal years $2024\le y\le d_c$. We first minimize the required peak annual renewal capacity $M$:
\begin{equation}
 \min M:\qquad \sum_{y\le d_c}a_{cy}=n_c,\quad
 \sum_c a_{cy}\le M,\quad a_{cy}\in\mathbb Z_+.
\end{equation}
With $M$ fixed, a second solve minimizes $\sum_{c,y}(d_c-y)a_{cy}$, avoiding avoidable early renewal. Among ties, a third solve prioritizes earlier non-electric conversion without changing either optimum. This is an executed integer program using the public cohort counts, not an assumed uniform purchase schedule.

An independent certificate follows from $D_t=\sum_{c:d_c\le t}n_c$:
\begin{equation}
 M^\star=\max_{t=2024,\ldots,2033}
 \left\lceil\frac{D_t}{t-2023}\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
Necessity follows because all $D_t$ slots must fit into $t-2023$ annual capacities. Sufficiency follows by allocating the earliest-deadline cohorts first when every cohort may renew from 2024. Later technical release dates, class-specific production limits and charging construction can increase the requirement; the certificate concerns this cohort submodel.

\section{Sensitivity, limitations and completion gate}
The executed London life-cap experiments show why retirement policy matters: reducing the cap from 17 to 14 years raises required peak annual renewal capacity by 25.9\%, while reducing the remaining-service exposure attributed to accelerated 2033 retirement. Chicago's older age profile produces a large first-year renewal condition. These age-only results do not value lost service life or certify winter operation.

For the unresolved operational baseline, solve a scenario grid over grant shares 0/25/50\%, delivered bus price, energy price and demand charges, grid intensity, electric winter kWh/km, battery degradation and replacement, payload loss, charger failures, depot construction delays, natural retirement and discount rate. Vary stakeholder trade-off weights only after computing the feasible cost--GHG frontier. Report intervals and threshold values: the lowest grid capacity, highest winter energy use or longest commissioning delay at which the 2033 target fails. State the exact block, depot, lost capacity and extra vehicle/charger need for every failure.

The strongest current limitation is structural, not a lack of algebra: public TransLink time-bin service means cannot reconstruct exact timetabled trips, vehicle blocks, deadhead, energy state or charger concurrency. The 2020 fleet table cannot prove the 2023 age stock. Historical 2019-CAD cost items are not comparable with an unrebased 2023 budget. The environmental inventory requires matched production, infrastructure and electricity boundaries. Filling any of these with unsourced single numbers would turn a result into a guess.

Before a decision-ready submission, three gates must pass: (i) reconstructed service and fleet reproduce published 2023 totals within disclosed tolerances; (ii) every candidate passes trip, payload, winter SOC, charging and site constraints, including 2033 accessory power; and (iii) every cost and life-cycle emission entry has a source, year, unit and uncertainty range. \section*{Letter to a Metro Vancouver decision-maker}
\textbf{To:} TransLink Board and Mayors' Council\\
\textbf{Subject:} Conditions for a service-preserving all-electric fixed-route bus fleet by 2033

Dear decision-makers,

Our analysis treats 2033 as an accelerated target relative to TransLink's published 2040 zero-emission goal. We would preserve every 2023 fixed-route departure, peak passenger capacity, accessibility and spare-bus provision throughout the transition. Existing trolleybuses already provide electric service and should be included in the baseline.

The public 2023 route data account for approximately 98.0\% of independently reported actual fixed-route bus hours. We also know the 2023 total of 106.87 million bus service km and 5.72 billion capacity km. These are useful calibration targets, but they do not identify individual bus duties, fleet ages, charging opportunities or depot electrical limits. Consequently, no responsible analysis can currently certify the number of replacement buses or chargers needed to reach 2033, or quote a defensible city-wide cost and emissions score. A battery bus with a diesel heater would also fail the strict fully electric definition used here.

We recommend an audited 2023 vehicle and trip-block register, followed by all-electric winter trials. Standard buses on verified duties are the first procurement group. Articulated duties follow after capacity and winter-range checks. Double-deck and community-shuttle commitments should wait for demonstrated all-day electric operation and commissioned depot capacity. The enclosed annual roadmap makes each expansion conditional on those tests rather than relying on a one-for-one replacement assumption.

Evaluate each proposed purchase against ordinary fleet renewal with identical service. Report total project resource cost separately from TransLink's payments after 0\%, 25\% and 50\% grants. Present both lifetime greenhouse-gas emissions and local tailpipe pollution; report battery and infrastructure manufacturing separately. Retain negative economic scores if the cleaner option costs more. A grant can improve affordability without erasing its resource cost.

Appraise electrical capacity and tariffs jointly: a zero billed nighttime peak still requires physical grid capacity. Our charging bounds show that a constrained site can reverse the preferred tariff. The decisive next step is to make the operating data available to the planning team. If winter range, vehicle payload or depot interconnection prevents any 2033 duty from being fully electric, disclose the precise gap and the additional vehicle, charger or construction condition needed. This gives the region a credible decision, even if the deadline itself proves infeasible.

Sincerely,\\
Fleet Transition Study Team
